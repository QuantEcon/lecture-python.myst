# Merging of Opinions: The Blackwell–Dubins Theorem

```{contents} Contents
:depth: 2
```

## Overview

This lecture studies the merging-of-opinions theorem of {cite:t}`blackwell1962`.

The theorem asks a simple question:

> If two agents hold different prior beliefs about a stochastic process but observe the same stream of data indefinitely, will their probability assessments eventually converge?

The answer is yes under an absolute-continuity condition.

If $Q \ll P$ (that is, $P$ dominates $Q$), then the conditional distributions under $P$ and $Q$ over the entire future path merge in total variation, $Q$-almost surely.

If in addition $P \ll Q$ (so that $P \sim Q$), then the same conclusion holds under both agents' probabilities.

This result connects to several other ideas:

- Bayesian consistency: posterior predictions approach the truth when the prior lies in the right absolute-continuity class ({doc}`likelihood_bayes`).
- Agreement results: {cite:t}`aumann1976` studies agents with a common prior and private information; Blackwell–Dubins studies agents with different priors and common information.
- Kakutani's dichotomy: for product measures, equivalence versus singularity can be read from a Hellinger criterion.

We develop the theory in discrete time and then sketch the continuous-time analogue.

Throughout, we use the Beta–Bernoulli model as a running example.

Two agents observe the same stream of coin flips but start from different priors over the coin's bias.

Let us start with some imports.

## Probability measures on sequence spaces

### The sequence space and its filtration

Let $(S, \mathscr{S})$ be a standard Borel space (i.e., a measurable space isomorphic to a Borel subset of a complete separable metric space), called the signal space.

The standard Borel assumption guarantees the existence of regular conditional distributions, which the theorem requires.

Set $\Omega = S^{\mathbb{N}}$, the set of all infinite sequences
$\omega = (x_1, x_2, \ldots)$ with $x_n \in S$, equipped with the product
$\sigma$-algebra $\mathscr{F} = \mathscr{S}^{\otimes \mathbb{N}}$.

For each $n \geq 1$, define the *finite-horizon* $\sigma$-algebra

$$
\mathscr{F}_n = \sigma(x_1, \ldots, x_n),
$$

and set $\mathscr{F}_0 = \{\emptyset, \Omega\}$, so $\mathscr{F}_0 \subseteq \mathscr{F}_1 \subseteq \mathscr{F}_2 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq \mathscr{F}$.

Define $\mathscr{F}_\infty = \sigma\!\left(\bigcup_{n \geq 1} \mathscr{F}_n\right)$, the $\sigma$-algebra of events that can eventually be learned.

Cylinder sets $\{x_1 \in A_1, \ldots, x_n \in A_n\}$ generate the product $\sigma$-algebra and belong to $\bigcup_n \mathscr{F}_n$, so $\mathscr{F}_\infty = \mathscr{F}$.

The $\sigma$-algebra $\mathscr{F}_\infty$ differs from the **tail $\sigma$-algebra** $\mathscr{T} = \bigcap_{n \geq 1} \sigma(x_n, x_{n+1}, \ldots)$, whose events, such as $\{x_n \in A \text{ for infinitely many } n\}$, do not depend on any finite stretch of data.

The collection $\{\mathscr{F}_n\}_{n \geq 1}$ is the **natural filtration**
generated by the observation process; $\mathscr{F}_n$ encodes everything
that can be learned from the first $n$ data points.

Let $P$ and $Q$ denote two probability measures on $(\Omega, \mathscr{F})$.

Write $P_n = P|_{\mathscr{F}_n}$ and $Q_n = Q|_{\mathscr{F}_n}$ for their
restrictions to the history up to time $n$.

### Absolute continuity

```{prf:definition} Absolute Continuity
:label: absolute_continuity

$P$ is **absolutely continuous** with respect to $Q$, written $P \ll Q$, if
$Q(A) = 0$ implies $P(A) = 0$ for every $A \in \mathscr{F}$.

They are **mutually absolutely continuous**, or **equivalent**, written $P \sim Q$,
if both $P \ll Q$ and $Q \ll P$.

$P$ is **locally absolutely continuous** with respect to $Q$ if $P_n \ll Q_n$
for every $n \geq 1$.
```

Global absolute continuity $P \ll Q$ implies local absolute continuity, but
not conversely.

Mutual absolute continuity means the two agents agree on which events are *possible*.

They can disagree about probabilities, but neither agent rules out an event the other deems possible.

### Total variation distance

```{prf:definition} Total Variation Distance
:label: total_variation_distance

For two probability measures $\mu$ and $\nu$ on $(E, \mathscr{E})$,

$$
\|\mu - \nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
= \sup_{A \in \mathscr{E}} |\mu(A) - \nu(A)|
= \frac{1}{2} \int_E \left|\frac{d\mu}{d\lambda} - \frac{d\nu}{d\lambda}\right| d\lambda,
$$

where $\lambda$ is any **dominating measure**, meaning $\mu \ll \lambda$ and $\nu \ll \lambda$ (for example, $\lambda = \mu + \nu$).

The distance $\|\mu - \nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}}$ lies in $[0,1]$; it equals 0 if and only if $\mu = \nu$ and 1 if and only if $\mu \perp \nu$ (mutual singularity).
```

When $\mu \ll \nu$ with $f = d\mu/d\nu$,

$$
\|\mu - \nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}} = \mathbb{E}_\nu[(f-1)^+] = 1 - \mathbb{E}_\nu[\min(f,1)].
$$

```{exercise}
:label: tv_derivation

Show the identity above.

*Hint:* Start from $\|\mu - \nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_\nu[|f - 1|]$ (which follows from taking $\nu$ as the dominating measure) and use the fact that $\mathbb{E}_\nu[f] = 1$.
```

```{solution} tv_derivation
:class: dropdown

Since $\mu \ll \nu$, we can use $\nu$ as the dominating measure, so $d\mu/d\nu = f$ and $d\nu/d\nu = 1$, giving

$$
\|\mu - \nu\|_{\mathrm{TV}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_\nu[|f - 1|].
$$

Write $|f-1| = (f-1)^+ + (1-f)^+$.

Since $\mu$ is a probability measure, $\mathbb{E}_\nu[f] = 1$, so the two parts contribute equally: $\mathbb{E}_\nu[(f-1)^+] = \mathbb{E}_\nu[(1-f)^+]$.

Therefore $\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_\nu[|f-1|] = \mathbb{E}_\nu[(f-1)^+]$.

Next, note that $(f-1)^+ = f - \min(f,1)$, so $\mathbb{E}_\nu[(f-1)^+] = \mathbb{E}_\nu[f] - \mathbb{E}_\nu[\min(f,1)] = 1 - \mathbb{E}_\nu[\min(f,1)]$.
```

Total variation is one of the strongest standard notions of distance between probability measures.

If two measures are close in total variation, then their probabilities of every event are close.

### The merging question

The Blackwell–Dubins theorem studies the conditional distribution of the *future* given the *past*.

At time $n$, after observing $(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$, each agent forms a conditional distribution over all future events:

$$
P(\,\cdot\,|\,\mathscr{F}_n)(\omega), \qquad
Q(\,\cdot\,|\,\mathscr{F}_n)(\omega).
$$

These are probability measures on the whole future path, not just the next observation.

The merging question asks whether

$$
d_n \;:=\; \bigl\|P(\,\cdot\,|\,\mathscr{F}_n) - Q(\,\cdot\,|\,\mathscr{F}_n)\bigr\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
\;\longrightarrow\; 0
$$

almost surely as $n \to \infty$.

At $n = 0$ the conditioning is trivial and $d_0 = \|P - Q\|_{\mathrm{TV}}$.


## The likelihood-ratio martingale

Our main tool is the Radon–Nikodym derivative process.

### The likelihood ratio

Since $Q \ll P$ implies $Q_n \ll P_n$ for every $n$, the Radon–Nikodym
theorem guarantees the existence of the likelihood ratio

$$
Z_n = \frac{dQ_n}{dP_n}, \qquad Z_n \geq 0 \;\; P\text{-a.s.},
\qquad \mathbb{E}_P[Z_n] = 1.
$$

The key structural property is that global absolute continuity $Q \ll P$
implies the existence of an overall Radon–Nikodym derivative $Z = dQ/dP$
on all of $(\Omega, \mathscr{F})$, and

$$
Z_n = \mathbb{E}_P[Z \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n] \qquad P\text{-a.s.}
$$

That is, $\{Z_n, \mathscr{F}_n\}_{n \geq 0}$ is a non-negative, uniformly
integrable $P$-martingale with $Z_0 = 1$.

Throughout the lecture, $Z$ and $Z_n$ denote densities of $Q$ with respect to $P$.

Posterior probabilities are martingales of the same kind; {doc}`blackwell_kihlstrom` uses that property to compare experiments.

```{prf:lemma} Martingale Convergence
:label: martingale_convergence

The likelihood-ratio process $\{Z_n\}$ satisfies:

1. $Z_n \to Z_\infty$ $P$-almost surely as $n \to \infty$.
2. $Z_\infty = \mathbb{E}_P[Z \,|\, \mathscr{F}_\infty] = Z$ $P$-a.s.
3. $Z_n \to Z_\infty$ in $L^1(P)$: $\;\mathbb{E}_P[|Z_n - Z_\infty|] \to 0$.

*Proof sketch.*  Non-negativity and the martingale property give boundedness
in $L^1(P)$.

Then almost-sure convergence follows from Doob's martingale
convergence theorem {cite:p}`doob1953`.

Uniform integrability (which follows
from $Z \in L^1(P)$ via the conditional Jensen inequality) upgrades this to
$L^1(P)$ convergence.

Lévy's upward theorem identifies the limit as $\mathbb{E}_P[Z \,|\, \mathscr{F}_\infty]$, which equals $Z$ because $\mathscr{F}_\infty = \mathscr{F}$. $\square$
```

From here on we write $Z_\infty$ for $Z = dQ/dP$.

### Connecting conditional measures to the likelihood ratio

The following identity connects the likelihood ratio to the conditional distributions.

On the set $\{Z_n > 0\}$, the Radon–Nikodym derivative of
$Q(\,\cdot\,|\,\mathscr{F}_n)$ with respect to $P(\,\cdot\,|\,\mathscr{F}_n)$
is

$$
\frac{d\,Q(\,\cdot\,|\,\mathscr{F}_n)}{d\,P(\,\cdot\,|\,\mathscr{F}_n)}
= \frac{Z_\infty}{Z_n}
\qquad P\text{-a.s. on } \{Z_n > 0\}.
$$

This is the abstract Bayes formula $Q(A \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n) = \mathbb{E}_P[Z_\infty \mathbf{1}_A \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n] / \mathbb{E}_P[Z_\infty \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n]$.

The set $\{Z_n > 0\}$ has $Q$-probability one, because $Q(Z_n = 0) = \mathbb{E}_P[Z_n \mathbf{1}\{Z_n = 0\}] = 0$.

Applying the total-variation formula with $f = Z_\infty / Z_n$ then gives

$$
d_n
= \mathbb{E}_{P(\cdot|\mathscr{F}_n)}\!\left[\left(\frac{Z_\infty}{Z_n} - 1\right)^{\!+}\right]
= 1 - \mathbb{E}_{P(\cdot|\mathscr{F}_n)}\!\left[\min\!\left(\frac{Z_\infty}{Z_n},\,1\right)\right].
$$

Because $Z_n$ is $\mathscr{F}_n$-measurable, the same quantity is a ratio of conditional expectations:

$$
d_n
= \frac{\mathbb{E}_P[(Z_\infty - Z_n)^+ \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n]}{Z_n}
= \frac{\mathbb{E}_P[|Z_\infty - Z_n| \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n]}{2 Z_n}
\qquad Q\text{-a.s.}
$$ (eq_dn_conditional)

The second equality holds because $\mathbb{E}_P[Z_\infty - Z_n \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n] = 0$, so the positive and negative parts of $Z_\infty - Z_n$ have equal conditional means.

Multiplying through by $Z_n$ and taking the $P$-expectation (then using $\mathbb{E}_P[Z_n \, g] = \mathbb{E}_Q[g]$ for non-negative $\mathscr{F}_n$-measurable $g$):

$$
2\,\mathbb{E}_Q[d_n] \;=\; \mathbb{E}_P[|Z_\infty - Z_n|].
$$

So the $L^1(P)$ convergence of the martingale gives $\mathbb{E}_Q[d_n] \to 0$, and its rate governs how fast $\mathbb{E}_Q[d_n]$ goes to zero.


## The Blackwell–Dubins theorem

```{prf:theorem} Blackwell–Dubins (1962)
:label: blackwell_dubins

Let $P$ and $Q$ be probability measures on $(\Omega, \mathscr{F})$ with
$Q \ll P$.

Define

$$
d_n = \bigl\|P(\,\cdot\,|\,\mathscr{F}_n) - Q(\,\cdot\,|\,\mathscr{F}_n)\bigr\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.
$$

Then $d_n \to 0$ $Q$-almost surely.
```

The identity $\mathbb{E}_Q[d_n] = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_P[|Z_\infty - Z_n|] \to 0$ gives convergence in $Q$-probability.

The proof of almost-sure convergence has three steps.

Step 1. A formula for $d_n$.

Equation {eq}`eq_dn_conditional` writes $d_n$ as the ratio of $\mathbb{E}_P[|Z_\infty - Z_n| \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n]$ to $2 Z_n$, $Q$-a.s.

Step 2. The denominator converges to a positive limit.

By {prf:ref}`martingale_convergence`, $Z_n \to Z_\infty$ $P$-a.s., hence $Q$-a.s. because $Q \ll P$.

The limit is positive $Q$-a.s., because $Q(Z_\infty = 0) = \mathbb{E}_P[Z_\infty \mathbf{1}\{Z_\infty = 0\}] = 0$.

Step 3. The numerator converges to zero.

Fix $M > 0$ and split $Z_\infty = U + V$, where $U = \min(Z_\infty, M)$ and $V = (Z_\infty - M)^+$.

Let $U_n = \mathbb{E}_P[U \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n]$ and $V_n = \mathbb{E}_P[V \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n]$, so $Z_n = U_n + V_n$.

The triangle inequality gives $|Z_\infty - Z_n| \leq |U - U_n| + V + V_n$, and conditioning on $\mathscr{F}_n$ gives

$$
\mathbb{E}_P[|Z_\infty - Z_n| \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n]
\leq \mathbb{E}_P[|U - U_n| \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n] + 2 V_n .
$$

Lévy's upward theorem gives $V_n \to V$ $P$-a.s.

For the bounded part, fix $m$ and let $W_m = \sup_{k \geq m} |U - U_k| \leq 2M$.

For $n \geq m$, $\mathbb{E}_P[|U - U_n| \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n] \leq \mathbb{E}_P[W_m \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n]$, and Lévy's upward theorem sends the right side to $W_m$ as $n \to \infty$.

Lévy's upward theorem also gives $U_k \to U$ $P$-a.s., so $W_m \to 0$ as $m \to \infty$.

Combining these limits,

$$
\limsup_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{E}_P[|Z_\infty - Z_n| \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n] \leq 2\,(Z_\infty - M)^+
\qquad P\text{-a.s.}
$$

for every $M$.

Letting $M \to \infty$ through the integers sends the right side to zero, because $Z_\infty$ is finite.

So the numerator converges to zero $P$-a.s., hence $Q$-a.s.

Steps 1–3 give $d_n \to 0$ $Q$-a.s. $\square$

The argument for the bounded part $U$ is Hunt's lemma.

The proof uses no monotonicity of $\{d_n\}$; a {ref}`later section <mo_supermartingale>` shows that $\{d_n\}$ need not be a supermartingale.

```{prf:remark} One-Sided vs. Mutual Absolute Continuity
:label: one_sided_vs_mutual

The theorem requires only $Q \ll P$, not $P \ll Q$.

One-sided absolute continuity $Q \ll P$ gives merging $Q$-almost surely.  

Since $Q \ll P$ means every $P$-null set is also $Q$-null, $Q$-a.s. convergence does *not* automatically imply $P$-a.s. convergence.

To conclude that $d_n \to 0$ under *both* agents' measures, one needs mutual absolute continuity $P \sim Q$.  

With $P \ll Q$ added, the proof can be run with the roles of $P$ and $Q$ swapped, yielding $d_n \to 0$ $P$-a.s. as well.
```

```{prf:remark} Sharpness
:label: sharpness

Absolute continuity matters.

When $P$ and $Q$ are singular, merging can fail completely.

Suppose $P \perp Q$ and $P_n \sim Q_n$ for every $n$.

Take $S \in \mathscr{F}$ with $P(S) = 1$ and $Q(S) = 0$.

Then $P(S \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n) = 1$ $P$-a.s. and $Q(S \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n) = 0$ $Q$-a.s.

The events $\{P(S \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n) = 1\}$ and $\{Q(S \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n) = 0\}$ belong to $\mathscr{F}_n$, so local equivalence makes each hold both $P$-a.s. and $Q$-a.s.

Hence $d_n \geq |P(S \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n) - Q(S \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n)| = 1$ for every $n$, under both measures.

The point-mass example below has $d_n = 1$ for every $n$.

For product measures, Kakutani's theorem later gives a sharp equivalence-versus-singularity dichotomy.
```


## The Beta–Bernoulli model

Before turning to Python, we introduce the main example used throughout
the simulations.

### Model

Suppose the data stream $(x_1, x_2, \ldots)$ consists of IID Bernoulli
draws with unknown probability $p^* \in (0,1)$.  

Agent $i$ has a Beta prior:

$$
p \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_i, \beta_i), \qquad i = 1, 2.
$$

After observing $n$ draws with $k$ successes, Bayes' rule yields the
posterior

$$
p \,|\, x^n \;\sim\; \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_i + k,\; \beta_i + n - k),
$$

and the one-step-ahead predictive probability is

$$
\hat{p}_i^n = \mathbb{E}[p\,|\,x^n] = \frac{\alpha_i + k}{\alpha_i + \beta_i + n}.
$$

By the strong law of large numbers, $k/n \to p^*$ almost surely, so both
$\hat{p}_1^n$ and $\hat{p}_2^n$ converge to $p^*$ regardless of the
agents' initial priors $(\alpha_i, \beta_i)$.

### The marginal likelihood and likelihood ratio

For each fixed value of $p \in (0,1)$, let $P_p$ denote the IID Bernoulli$(p)$
probability law on infinite sequences.

Agent $i$ does not know $p$.

Instead, agent $i$ places the prior density $\pi_i$ on $p$, which induces a
probability measure $P_i$ on data sequences via

$$
P_i(A) = \int_0^1 P_p(A)\,\pi_i(p)\,dp
\qquad \text{for every event } A.
$$

So $P_i$ is the agent's marginal probability measure over
histories after averaging over uncertainty about $p$.

In particular, if $x^n$ is an exact observed history with $k$ successes, then
$P_i(x^n)$ means the probability that agent $i$ assigns to that history under
this mixture measure.

To compute it, start from the Beta density

$$
\pi_i(p)
= \frac{p^{\alpha_i - 1} (1-p)^{\beta_i - 1}}{B(\alpha_i, \beta_i)},
\qquad 0 < p < 1.
$$

Given $p$, the probability of that ordered history is $p^k (1-p)^{n-k}$.

Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_i(x^n)
&= \int_0^1 p^k (1-p)^{n-k} \pi_i(p)\, dp \\
&= \frac{1}{B(\alpha_i, \beta_i)}
\int_0^1 p^{\alpha_i + k - 1} (1-p)^{\beta_i + n - k - 1}\, dp \\
&= \frac{B(\alpha_i + k,\; \beta_i + n - k)}{B(\alpha_i,\, \beta_i)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

where $B(a,b) = \Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)/\Gamma(a+b)$ is the beta function.

This expression is the probability of the ordered history $x^n$.

It depends on the data only through the count $k$, so histories with the same number of successes receive the same probability.

The likelihood ratio at time $n$ is therefore

$$
Z_n = \frac{P_{1,n}(x^n)}{P_{2,n}(x^n)}
= \frac{B(\alpha_2,\, \beta_2)}{B(\alpha_1,\, \beta_1)}
\cdot
\frac{B(\alpha_1 + k,\, \beta_1 + n - k)}{B(\alpha_2 + k,\, \beta_2 + n - k)}.
$$

In the notation of the theorem, $Q = P_1$ and $P = P_2$.

The process $\{Z_n\}$ is a martingale under $P_2$ (agent 2's probability) and converges
almost surely to a finite positive limit $Z_\infty$, reflecting the fact
that $P_1 \sim P_2$ for any Beta priors with positive parameters.

The limit has a closed form.

Let $\bar p = \lim_n k_n/n$, which exists $P_i$-a.s. because, conditional on $p$, the strong law of large numbers applies.

Then

$$
Z_\infty = \frac{dP_1}{dP_2} = \frac{\pi_1(\bar p)}{\pi_2(\bar p)}.
$$

{ref}`mo_ex_zinf` asks for a derivation.

For the priors $\mathrm{Beta}(1, 8)$ and $\mathrm{Beta}(8, 1)$ used below, $\pi_1(p) = 8(1-p)^7$ and $\pi_2(p) = 8p^7$, so

$$
\log Z_\infty = 7 \log \frac{1 - \bar p}{\bar p}.
$$

### The exact Blackwell–Dubins distance

For the Beta–Bernoulli model, there is a clean formula for $d_n$.

By construction, agent $i$'s conditional distribution of the future
sequence $(x_{n+1}, x_{n+2}, \ldots)$ given $x^n$ is a mixture of IID Bernoulli$(p)$
processes, with $p$ drawn from agent $i$'s posterior Beta distribution.

{doc}`exchangeable` describes de Finetti's theorem, which delivers such a mixture for every exchangeable process; here the mixture holds by construction.

The Bernoulli$(p)^{\infty}$ measures for different $p$ are mutually
singular, and the limiting frequency $\bar p$ recovers $p$.

The map $\omega \mapsto \bar p$ sends each agent's conditional distribution of the future to that agent's posterior, so the TV distance between the conditional distributions is at least the TV distance between the posteriors.

Each conditional distribution is the image of a posterior under the Markov kernel $p \mapsto \mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)^{\infty}$, so the TV distance between the conditional distributions is at most the TV distance between the posteriors.

Therefore

$$
d_n
= \bigl\|\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_1 + k_n,\,\beta_1 + n - k_n)
- \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_2 + k_n,\,\beta_2 + n - k_n)\bigr\|_{\mathrm{TV}}.
$$

Both posteriors concentrate around $k_n/n$ with standard deviations of order $n^{-1/2}$.

Concentration alone does not deliver $d_n \to 0$.

Two distributions with standard deviation $s_n$ have a TV distance that goes to zero only if their means differ by $o(s_n)$.

When $\alpha_1 + \beta_1 = \alpha_2 + \beta_2 = 9$, as below, the posterior means differ by $\delta_n = (\alpha_2 - \alpha_1)/(n + 9) = 7/(n+9)$, which is $O(1/n)$.

Both posterior standard deviations are approximately $s_n = \sqrt{\hat p_n (1 - \hat p_n)/n}$, where $\hat p_n = k_n/n$.

Approximating both posteriors by normal distributions with common standard deviation $s_n$ and means $\delta_n$ apart gives

$$
d_n \approx 2\,\Phi\!\left(\frac{\delta_n}{2 s_n}\right) - 1
\approx \frac{7}{\sqrt{2 \pi n\, \hat p_n (1 - \hat p_n)}},
$$

where $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF.

At $\hat p_n = 0.65$ the right side is $5.85/\sqrt{n}$.

The section on the rate of merging checks this approximation.

When $\alpha_1 + \beta_1 = \alpha_2 + \beta_2$, the ratio of the two posterior densities is proportional to $((1-p)/p)^{\beta_1 - \beta_2}$, which is monotone in $p$.

The two densities cross once, at a point $x^*$, and $d_n = |F_1(x^*) - F_2(x^*)|$, where $F_i$ is agent $i$'s posterior CDF.

The following code implements the Beta–Bernoulli updating, predictive probabilities, TV distance, and likelihood-ratio computations described above.

```{code-cell} ipython3
def beta_bernoulli_update(data, a0, b0):
    """
    Sequential Beta-Bernoulli Bayesian updating.
    """
    n = len(data)
    cum_k = np.concatenate([[0], np.cumsum(data)])   # cumulative successes
    ns    = np.arange(n + 1)                          # 0, 1, ..., n
    a_post = a0 + cum_k
    b_post = b0 + (ns - cum_k)
    return a_post, b_post


def predictive_prob(a_post, b_post):
    """One-step-ahead predictive probability P(X=1 | data)."""
    return a_post / (a_post + b_post)


def tv_distance_beta(a1, b1, a2, b2):
    """
    Exact TV distance between Beta(a1, b1) and Beta(a2, b2)
    when a1 + b1 = a2 + b2 and a1 != a2.

    The densities cross once, at x_star, where
    log(x_star / (1 - x_star))
        = (betaln(a2, b2) - betaln(a1, b1)) / (b1 - b2).
    Works elementwise on arrays.
    """
    logit_x = (betaln(a2, b2) - betaln(a1, b1)) / (b1 - b2)
    x_star = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-logit_x))
    return np.abs(beta_dist.cdf(x_star, a1, b1)
                  - beta_dist.cdf(x_star, a2, b2))


def log_likelihood_ratio(data, a1, b1, a2, b2):
    """
    Log likelihood ratio log Z_n = log P1_n(data) - log P2_n(data)
    for every prefix of `data`.

    Returns an array of length len(data) + 1, starting at 0 (before data).
    """
    a1p, b1p = beta_bernoulli_update(data, a1, b1)
    a2p, b2p = beta_bernoulli_update(data, a2, b2)
    log_P1 = betaln(a1p, b1p) - betaln(a1, b1)
    log_P2 = betaln(a2p, b2p) - betaln(a2, b2)
    return log_P1 - log_P2


def run_simulation(p_true, a1, b1, a2, b2, n_steps, seed=0):
    """
    Simulate one realisation of the merging experiment.

    Returns a dict with arrays of length n_steps + 1 (index 0 = prior).
    """
    rng  = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    data = rng.binomial(1, p_true, n_steps)

    a1p, b1p = beta_bernoulli_update(data, a1, b1)
    a2p, b2p = beta_bernoulli_update(data, a2, b2)

    pred1    = predictive_prob(a1p, b1p)
    pred2    = predictive_prob(a2p, b2p)
    tv_1step = np.abs(pred1 - pred2)

    # TV between posterior Betas; in this model this equals d_n
    tv_beta = tv_distance_beta(a1p, b1p, a2p, b2p)

    log_Z = log_likelihood_ratio(data, a1, b1, a2, b2)

    return dict(data=data, pred1=pred1, pred2=pred2,
                tv_1step=tv_1step, tv_beta=tv_beta, log_Z=log_Z)
```

### Simulation

We choose two agents with very different beliefs about the bias of a coin.

- Agent 1 (skeptic): prior $\mathrm{Beta}(1, 8)$, so
  $\hat{p}_1^0 = 1/9 \approx 0.11$.
- Agent 2 (optimist): prior $\mathrm{Beta}(8, 1)$, so
  $\hat{p}_2^0 = 8/9 \approx 0.89$.

Both priors are supported on all of $(0,1)$, so $P_1 \sim P_2$.

Blackwell–Dubins guarantees merging on $P_1$-almost every path and on $P_2$-almost every path.

The figure below follows one path generated by a coin with fixed probability of heads $p^* = 0.65$.

The law $P_{0.65}$ of such a path is singular with respect to $P_1$ and $P_2$: the event $\{k_n/n \to 0.65\}$ has $P_{0.65}$-probability one and $P_i$-probability zero.

Blackwell–Dubins therefore says nothing about this path.

Merging at a fixed $p^*$ follows from the Bernstein–von Mises theorem.

Under $P_{p^*}$, each agent's posterior is close in total variation to the normal distribution with mean $k_n/n$ and variance $p^*(1-p^*)/n$, so the two posteriors are close to each other.

The normal approximation above makes this concrete.

### Is $d_n$ a supermartingale?

The proof of the theorem uses no monotonicity of $\{d_n\}$.

In general, $\{d_n\}$ is neither a $P$-supermartingale nor a $Q$-supermartingale.

A five-point example shows this.

Let $\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$, let $P$ be uniform, and let $Z_\infty = dQ/dP = (0, 1, 1, 1, 2)$.

A first observation reveals whether $\omega \in \{1, 2\}$ or $\omega \in \{3, 4, 5\}$, and a second observation reveals $\omega$.

```{code-cell} ipython3
P_pts = np.full(5, 0.2)
Q_pts = np.array([0, 1, 1, 1, 2]) * P_pts
blocks = [[0, 1], [2, 3, 4]]      # cells revealed by the first observation

d_0 = 0.5 * np.abs(P_pts - Q_pts).sum()
E_Q_d1 = E_P_d1 = 0.0
for b in blocks:
    P_b, Q_b = P_pts[b].sum(), Q_pts[b].sum()
    d_1 = 0.5 * np.abs(P_pts[b] / P_b - Q_pts[b] / Q_b).sum()
    E_Q_d1 += Q_b * d_1
    E_P_d1 += P_b * d_1

print(f"d_0 = {d_0:.3f}, E_Q[d_1] = {E_Q_d1:.3f}, E_P[d_1] = {E_P_d1:.3f}")
```

Here $d_0 = 0.200$, $\mathbb{E}_Q[d_1] = 0.233$, and $\mathbb{E}_P[d_1] = 0.300$.

The first observation raises the expected distance under both measures.

The second observation reveals $\omega$, so $d_2 = 0$.

The identity $2\,\mathbb{E}_Q[d_n] = \mathbb{E}_P[|Z_\infty - Z_n|]$ locates the source of the failure: the $L^1$ distance between $Z_\infty$ and its conditional expectation given $\mathscr{F}_n$ need not decrease with $n$.

When $\mathbb{E}_P[Z_\infty^2] < \infty$, the $L^2$ distance does decrease, because $\mathbb{E}_P[(Z_\infty - Z_n)^2] = \mathbb{E}_P[Z_\infty^2] - \mathbb{E}_P[Z_n^2]$ and $\mathbb{E}_P[Z_n^2]$ is nondecreasing in $n$.

In the Beta–Bernoulli model with the priors above, the inequality $\mathbb{E}[d_{n+1} \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n] \leq d_n$ holds at every state we check.

The next cell computes $\mathbb{E}_Q[d_{n+1} \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n] - d_n$ and $\mathbb{E}_P[d_{n+1} \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n] - d_n$ exactly at every state $(n, k_n)$ with $n < 400$, using each agent's one-step predictive probability.

```{code-cell} ipython3
    E_Q[d_{n+1} | n, k] - d_n and E_P[d_{n+1} | n, k] - d_n
```

## Failure of merging: mutual singularity

What happens when the hypothesis $Q \ll P$ fails?

The singular case is the cleanest counterexample.

### Point-mass priors

Suppose both agents hold degenerate (point-mass) priors:

- Agent P: certain that $p = p_P = 0.30$.
- Agent Q: certain that $p = p_Q = 0.75$.

Since $P$ charges only sequences whose empirical frequency converges to $0.30$, and $Q$ charges only sequences whose empirical frequency converges to $0.75$, the two measures are mutually singular: $P \perp Q$.

The conditional distributions do not update, because both agents are already certain of their model.

For the theorem's object, namely the conditional law of the entire future path,

$$
\|P(\,\cdot\,|\,\mathscr{F}_n) - Q(\,\cdot\,|\,\mathscr{F}_n)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
= \|P - Q\|_{\mathrm{TV}} = 1
\quad \text{for all } n.
$$

This equality holds because the infinite-product Bernoulli measures with distinct success probabilities are singular.

If we look only one step ahead, the predictive distance is $|p_P - p_Q| = 0.45$.

That is smaller than one, but it is not the quantity that appears in Blackwell–Dubins.

## Kakutani's theorem: when does merging hold?

A natural question is: for which product measures does the Blackwell–Dubins
hypothesis $Q \ll P$ hold?

For infinite product measures, the answer is
given by a classical result of {cite:t}`kakutani1948`.

### Hellinger affinities

In this section $P^{(m)}$ and $Q^{(m)}$ denote probability measures on the signal space $(S, \mathscr{S})$, the factors of product measures.

The notation $P_n$ and $Q_n$ keeps its earlier meaning: restrictions of $P$ and $Q$ to $\mathscr{F}_n$.

```{prf:definition} Hellinger Affinity
:label: hellinger_affinity

For probability measures $P^{(m)}$ and $Q^{(m)}$ on $(S, \mathscr{S})$ with common
dominating measure $\lambda$, the **Hellinger affinity** is

$$
\rho_m = \int_S \sqrt{\frac{dP^{(m)}}{d\lambda} \cdot \frac{dQ^{(m)}}{d\lambda}}\,d\lambda
\;\in\; [0, 1].
$$

$\rho_m = 1$ if and only if $P^{(m)} = Q^{(m)}$; $\rho_m = 0$ if and only if $P^{(m)} \perp Q^{(m)}$.
```

The Hellinger affinity is the Bhattacharyya coefficient, the Chernoff coefficient at $\phi = 1/2$ in {doc}`divergence_measures`.

For two specific one-dimensional families:

- Gaussian: $P^{(m)} = \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ vs $Q^{(m)} = \mathcal{N}(\mu_m,1)$:

$$
\rho_m^{\text{Gauss}} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\mu_m^2}{8}\right).
$$

- Bernoulli: $P^{(m)} = \mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)$ vs $Q^{(m)} = \mathrm{Bernoulli}(q)$:

$$
\rho_m^{\text{Bern}} = \sqrt{pq} + \sqrt{(1-p)(1-q)}.
$$

### Kakutani's dichotomy

```{prf:theorem} Kakutani (1948)
:label: kakutani_dichotomy

Let $P = \bigotimes_{m=1}^\infty P^{(m)}$ and $Q = \bigotimes_{m=1}^\infty Q^{(m)}$
be infinite product measures whose factors are pairwise equivalent: $P^{(m)} \sim Q^{(m)}$ for every $m$.

Then either $P \sim Q$ or $P \perp Q$; there
is no intermediate case.

Specifically,

$$
P \sim Q
\quad \iff \quad
\prod_{m=1}^\infty \rho_m > 0
\quad \iff \quad
\sum_{m=1}^\infty (1 - \rho_m) < \infty.
$$

If $\prod_{m=1}^\infty \rho_m = 0$, then $P \perp Q$.

*Proof idea.*
A standard proof studies the likelihood-ratio martingale
$Z_N = \prod_{m=1}^N (dQ^{(m)}/dP^{(m)})$ together with the identity
$\mathbb{E}_P[\sqrt{Z_N}] = \prod_{m=1}^N \rho_m$.

If the product is positive, $\{\sqrt{Z_N}\}$ is Cauchy in $L^2(P)$, so $Z_N$ converges in $L^1(P)$ and $Q \ll P$; the same argument with $P$ and $Q$ exchanged gives $P \ll Q$.

If the product is zero, Fatou's lemma gives $\mathbb{E}_P[\sqrt{Z_\infty}] = 0$, so $Z_\infty = 0$ $P$-a.s. and $P \perp Q$.

$\square$
```

### Implication for merging

For IID-type sequences, Kakutani's theorem gives the following picture:

| Scenario | $\sum_m (1-\rho_m)$ | Conclusion | Merging? |
|---|---|---|---|
| $P^{(m)} = Q^{(m)}$ for all $m$ | $0$ | $P = Q$ | Trivially yes |
| $P^{(m)} \ne Q^{(m)}$ with $\sum_m (1-\rho_m) < \infty$ | Finite | $P \sim Q$ | Yes; Blackwell–Dubins applies |
| $P^{(m)} = f \ne g = Q^{(m)}$ for all $m$ | $\infty$ | $P \perp Q$ | No |

The IID case with different fixed marginals is the standard no-merging example.

If two agents assign permanently different distributions to each observation, they end up in disjoint probability worlds.

{doc}`likelihood_ratio_process` shows the likelihood ratio process converging to zero when nature permanently draws from one of two fixed densities.

For product measures, the conditional distribution of the future given $\mathscr{F}_n$ does not depend on the past.

So $d_n = \bigl\|\bigotimes_{m>n} P^{(m)} - \bigotimes_{m>n} Q^{(m)}\bigr\|_{\mathrm{TV}}$ is deterministic.

Each $\rho_m$ is positive because the factors are equivalent, so dropping finitely many factors does not change whether $\prod_m \rho_m$ is positive.

When $P \perp Q$, the products over $m > n$ are singular, and $d_n = 1$ for every $n$.

When $P \sim Q$, $d_n \to 0$ by Blackwell–Dubins.

{ref}`mo_ex_gauss` computes $d_n$ exactly for Gaussian products.

### A Gaussian product-measure example

We illustrate Kakutani's dichotomy with Gaussian product measures.

Take $P = \mathcal{N}(0,1)^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}$ as the reference measure and $Q = \bigotimes_m \mathcal{N}(\mu_m,1)$ as the alternative.

3. $\mu_m = c/m$ ($\sum (1-\rho_m) \approx \sum c^2/(8m^2) < \infty$) $\Rightarrow P \sim Q$.

## Extension to continuous time

The same logic extends to continuous time.

### Girsanov's theorem and the likelihood-ratio process

On the canonical Wiener space with $P$ the Wiener measure (standard
Brownian motion $W$), suppose agent $Q$ believes the process has an
additional drift $\theta = \{\theta_s\}_{s \geq 0}$:

$$
W_t = \widetilde{W}_t + \int_0^t \theta_s\, ds,
$$

where $\widetilde{W}$ is a $Q$-Brownian motion.

The Girsanov–Cameron–Martin theorem {cite:p}`girsanov1960` gives the
likelihood-ratio process $Z_t = dQ_t/dP_t$ as the stochastic exponential

$$
Z_t
= \exp\!\left(\int_0^t \theta_s\, dW_s - \frac{1}{2}\int_0^t \theta_s^2\, ds\right).
$$

$Z_t$ is always a non-negative $P$-local martingale; it is a true martingale
if and only if $\mathbb{E}_P[Z_t] = 1$ for all $t$.

Novikov's condition {cite:p}`novikov1972`,
$\mathbb{E}_P\!\left[\exp\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\int_0^T \theta_s^2\,ds\right)\right] < \infty$ for all $T$,
is sufficient.

### The dichotomy at infinity

A key subtlety on $[0,+\infty)$ is that local absolute continuity does *not* imply global absolute continuity on $\mathscr{F}_\infty$.

```{prf:remark} Infinite-Horizon Subtlety
:label: dichotomy_at_infinity

Suppose $Z_t$ is a true $P$-martingale for every finite horizon and let $Z_t \to Z_\infty$ $P$-a.s.

If $\{Z_t\}$ is uniformly integrable on $[0,\infty)$, then $Q \ll P$ on $\mathscr{F}_\infty$ with $dQ/dP = Z_\infty$.

That is the hypothesis of Blackwell–Dubins, so merging holds $Q$-a.s.

If in addition $Z_\infty > 0$ $P$-a.s., then $P \ll Q$ as well, and merging holds $P$-a.s.

Merging $P$-a.s. requires only $P \ll Q$, because $d_t$ is symmetric in $P$ and $Q$ and the theorem applies with their roles exchanged.

If uniform integrability fails, then global absolute continuity on $\mathscr{F}_\infty$ can fail.

In many standard examples, including a non-zero constant drift, the measures are in fact singular on $\mathscr{F}_\infty$.
```

For a deterministic drift $\theta$, the **energy condition**

$$
\int_0^\infty \theta_s^2\,ds < \infty
$$

separates the two cases.

If it holds, $\mathbb{E}_P[Z_t^2] = \exp\!\left(\int_0^t \theta_s^2\, ds\right)$ is bounded in $t$, $\{Z_t\}$ is uniformly integrable, $Z_\infty > 0$, and $P \sim Q$ on $\mathscr{F}_\infty$.

If it fails, $\log Z_t \to -\infty$ $P$-a.s. and $P \perp Q$ on $\mathscr{F}_\infty$.

Informally, $\int_0^\infty \theta_s^2\,ds$ measures the total amount of information separating the two measures over the infinite horizon.

Under the energy condition, Blackwell–Dubins applies and merging holds under both measures.

When $\theta$ is a non-zero constant, the condition fails, the measures are singular on $\mathscr{F}_\infty$, and merging does not occur.

{doc}`survival_recursive_preferences` studies agents whose beliefs differ from the truth by a constant drift of this kind.

When $\theta$ depends on the path of $W$, $\int_0^\infty \theta_s^2\,ds$ is a random variable.

In that case $P \sim Q$ on $\mathscr{F}_\infty$ if and only if $\int_0^\infty \theta_s^2\,ds < \infty$ both $P$-a.s. and $Q$-a.s.

Under $P$, $\log Z_t = \int_0^t \theta_s\,dW_s - \tfrac{1}{2}\int_0^t \theta_s^2\,ds$ tends to $-\infty$ on the event $\{\int_0^\infty \theta_s^2\,ds = \infty\}$.

Under $Q$, $\log Z_t = \int_0^t \theta_s\,d\widetilde{W}_s + \tfrac{1}{2}\int_0^t \theta_s^2\,ds$ tends to $+\infty$ on the same event.

On the complementary event, $\log Z_t$ converges to a finite limit under both measures.

Since $Q \ll P$ on $\mathscr{F}_\infty$ if and only if $Z_\infty < \infty$ $Q$-a.s., and $P \ll Q$ if and only if $Z_\infty > 0$ $P$-a.s., the claim follows.

Whenever $Q \ll P$ on $\mathscr{F}_\infty$ is established, the discrete-time
proof carries over.

The conditional distance satisfies $d_t = \mathbb{E}_P[|Z_\infty - Z_t| \,|\, \mathscr{F}_t]/(2 Z_t)$, $Z_t \to Z_\infty > 0$ $Q$-a.s., and the truncation argument in Step 3 sends the numerator to zero.

The identity $\mathbb{E}_Q[d_t] = \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_P[|Z_t - Z_\infty|]$ also carries over.


## Applications

### Bayesian learning

The most direct application is Bayesian inference.

Suppose data $(x_1, x_2, \ldots)$ are drawn from the true measure $Q^*$.

An agent holds a prior $\pi$ over a family $\{Q_\theta : \theta \in \Theta\}$, inducing a marginal $P = \int Q_\theta\,\pi(d\theta)$.

If $Q^* \ll P$ (i.e., the agent's marginal model dominates the truth), then Blackwell–Dubins gives

$$
\bigl\|P(\,\cdot\,|\,x_1,\ldots,x_n) - Q^*(\,\cdot\,|\,x_1,\ldots,x_n)\bigr\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
\to 0 \quad Q^*\text{-a.s.}
$$

This is a strong form of Bayesian consistency: the agent's predictions merge with the truth under the true measure.

A prior assigning positive mass to a neighbourhood of the true parameter typically guarantees *local* absolute continuity $Q^*_n \ll P_n$ for every finite horizon $n$, but not the global condition $Q^* \ll P$ on $\mathscr{F}_\infty$ that Blackwell–Dubins requires.

For example, in the Beta–Bernoulli model with a non-atomic prior $\pi$, the mixture $P = \int \mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)^{\infty}\,\pi(dp)$ satisfies $Q^*_n \ll P_n$ for every $n$, yet $Q^* \not\ll P$ globally because the set $\{\lim k_n/n = p^*\}$ has $Q^*$-measure one but $P$-measure zero (different Bernoulli product measures are mutually singular).

In an identifiable IID family, the measures $Q_\theta$ for distinct $\theta$ are mutually singular, so $Q_{\theta^*} \ll P$ if and only if $\pi(\{\theta^*\}) > 0$.

Global absolute continuity therefore holds when $\pi$ has an atom at $\theta^*$, for example when $\Theta$ is finite.

{doc}`likelihood_bayes` studies an agent whose prior puts positive probability on each of two models, one of which is true; there $Q^* \ll P$.

With a non-atomic prior, the agent's forecasts of the entire future never merge with the truth.

In the Beta–Bernoulli model, the argument that gave the closed form for $d_n$ gives $\|P_i(\,\cdot\,|\,\mathscr{F}_n) - P_{p^*}(\,\cdot\,|\,\mathscr{F}_n)\|_{\mathrm{TV}} = 1$ for every $n$, because agent $i$'s posterior has no atom at $p^*$.

Each agent's one-step-ahead forecast still converges to $p^*$, and the two agents' forecasts merge with each other.

Doob's consistency theorem {cite:p}`doob1949` states that the posterior concentrates at the true $\theta$ for $\pi$-almost every $\theta$.

{cite:t}`DiaconisFreedman1986` study consistency at a fixed parameter value, where Doob's theorem is silent.

They show that in infinite-dimensional models the exceptional $\pi$-null set can matter: in a location problem with a Dirichlet-process prior on the error distribution, the posterior can be inconsistent at parameter values in the support of the prior.

In {doc}`mix_model`, nature draws IID from a mixture $h$ of densities $f$ and $g$, while the agent believes that nature drew once and for all from $f$ or from $g$.

There $P \perp Q^*$, the agent's forecasts stay wrong, and the agent's posterior concentrates on the model closer to $h$ in Kullback–Leibler divergence.

### Rational expectations and heterogeneous priors

In macroeconomics, rational-expectations models typically impose a common prior.

Blackwell–Dubins gives a dynamic justification for weaker initial agreement.

If two agents start with equivalent priors and observe the same history, their conditional forecasts eventually agree on every event.

{cite:t}`aumann1976`'s agreement theorem complements this result.

Aumann's agents share a common prior and see different private information; if their posterior probabilities of an event are common knowledge, the posteriors are equal.

Blackwell and Dubins's agents hold different priors and see the same information.

When merging fails, disagreement persists and has economic consequences.

{doc}`likelihood_ratio_process_2` studies how wealth moves between agents with different beliefs, including an agent whose prior rules out the true model.

{doc}`survival_recursive_preferences` studies which agents survive when beliefs differ permanently.

### Ergodic Markov chains

For a Markov chain with transition kernel $\Pi$ and two initial
distributions $\mu$ and $\nu$, the $n$-step distributions are $\mu\Pi^n$
and $\nu\Pi^n$.

If $\Pi$ is ergodic with unique stationary distribution
$\pi$, both converge to $\pi$, so

$$
\|\mu\Pi^n - \nu\Pi^n\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
\leq \|\mu\Pi^n - \pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}} + \|\nu\Pi^n - \pi\|_{\mathrm{TV}}
\to 0.
$$

This convergence does *not* require absolute continuity: $\mu$ and $\nu$ can be point masses at different states.

It concerns a different object from Blackwell–Dubins.

The measures $\mu\Pi^n$ and $\nu\Pi^n$ are unconditional distributions of the state at date $n$, and their convergence says that an ergodic chain forgets its initial condition.

Blackwell–Dubins concerns conditional distributions of the entire future given a common observed history.

Two agents who agree on $\Pi$ and observe the current state make identical conditional forecasts of the future, whether or not $\Pi$ is ergodic.


## The rate of merging

Blackwell–Dubins is qualitative.

It tells us that $d_n \to 0$, but not how fast.

The identity

$$
\mathbb{E}_Q[d_n] = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_P[|Z_n - Z_\infty|]
$$

shows that the rate of merging in $Q$-mean is governed by the $L^1(P)$ convergence rate of the likelihood-ratio martingale.

In regular parametric examples, one often sees $n^{-1/2}$-type behavior.

In the Beta–Bernoulli model, the normal approximation derived earlier gives $d_n \approx 7/\sqrt{2 \pi n\, \hat p_n (1 - \hat p_n)}$.

Under a fixed coin $P_{p^*}$, $\hat p_n \to p^*$, so $\mathbb{E}_{P_{p^*}}[d_n] \approx C(p^*)/\sqrt{n}$ with $C(p^*) = 7/\sqrt{2\pi p^*(1-p^*)}$ and $C(0.65) = 5.85$.

Under $Q = P_1$, the coin $p$ is drawn from $\pi_1$, and averaging over $p$ gives $\mathbb{E}_Q[d_n] \approx C_Q/\sqrt{n}$ with

$$
C_Q = \frac{7}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, \mathbb{E}_{\pi_1}\!\left[(p(1-p))^{-1/2}\right]
= \frac{7}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, \frac{B(1/2,\, 15/2)}{B(1,\, 8)} = 14.7 .
$$

The next cell computes both expectations exactly by summing $d_n$ over the number of successes $k_n$, which is binomial under $P_{p^*}$ and beta-binomial under $Q = P_1$.

## Summary and extensions

The logical flow underlying the Blackwell–Dubins theorem is:

$$
Q \ll P
\;\Longrightarrow\;
Z = \frac{dQ}{dP} \in L^1(P)
\;\Longrightarrow\;
Z_n = \mathbb{E}_P[Z \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n] \to Z
\text{ and }
\mathbb{E}_P[|Z - Z_n| \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n] \to 0
\;\; P\text{-a.s.}
\;\Longrightarrow\;
d_n = \frac{\mathbb{E}_P[|Z - Z_n| \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n]}{2 Z_n} \xrightarrow{Q\text{-a.s.}} 0.
$$

Takeaways:

1. One-sided absolute continuity $Q \ll P$ gives merging $Q$-almost surely.  For merging under *both* measures, one needs mutual absolute continuity $P \sim Q$.

2. The likelihood-ratio martingale $Z_n = \mathbb{E}_P[Z|\mathscr{F}_n]$ drives the result: in $d_n = \mathbb{E}_P[|Z - Z_n| \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n]/(2 Z_n)$, the numerator converges to zero and $Z_n$ converges to $Z > 0$, $Q$-a.s.

3. More data can only increase the ability to distinguish two hypotheses: $\|P_n - Q_n\|_{\mathrm{TV}}$ on $\mathscr{F}_n$ is nondecreasing in $n$. The conditional distance $d_n$ need not decrease, even in expectation. It converges to zero when $Q \ll P$.

4. For infinite product measures, Kakutani's theorem gives a sharp equivalence-versus-singularity dichotomy: either $P \sim Q$ (when $\sum_m (1 - \rho_m) < \infty$) or $P \perp Q$ (when the sum diverges), with no intermediate case.

5. When $P \sim Q$, Blackwell–Dubins applies and merging occurs under both measures; when $P \perp Q$ and $P_n \sim Q_n$ for every $n$, $d_n = 1$ for every $n$ and disagreement persists forever.

### Applications in economics

Some influential applications and extensions are:

- {cite}`KalaiLehrer1993Nash`: repeated-game learning drives play toward Nash behavior when priors are absolutely continuous with respect to the truth.
- {cite}`KalaiLehrer1993Subjective`: subjective and objective equilibria coincide asymptotically under the same condition.
- {cite}`KalaiLehrer1994Merging`: weak and strong notions of merging are introduced for environments where full total-variation convergence is too strong.
- {cite}`KalaiLehrerSmorodinsky1999`: merging is linked to calibrated forecasting.
- {cite}`JacksonKalaiSmorodinsky1999`: de Finetti-style representations are connected to Bayesian learning and posterior convergence.
- {cite}`JacksonKalai1999`: social learning erodes reputational effects that rely on persistent disagreement across cohorts.
- {cite}`Sandroni1998Nash`: near-absolute-continuity conditions are shown to suffice for Nash-type convergence in repeated games.
- {cite}`MillerSanchirico1999`: gives an alternative proof and an economic interpretation of persistent disagreement in terms of mutually favorable bets.
- {cite}`LehrerSmorodinsky1996Compatible`: studies broader compatibility notions beyond Blackwell--Dubins absolute continuity.

Part 1.

The density of $\pi_1$ is $2\cdot\mathbf{1}\{p < \tfrac12\} \leq 2\pi_2(p)$, so $P_1(A) \leq 2 P_2(A)$ for every event $A$, and $P_1 \ll P_2$.

Let $\bar p = \limsup_n k_n/n$.

The event $\{\bar p > \tfrac12\}$ has $P_2$-probability $\tfrac12$ and $P_1$-probability zero, so $P_2 \ll P_1$ fails.

{ref}`mo_ex_zinf` gives $dP_1/dP_2 = \pi_1(\bar p)/\pi_2(\bar p) = 2\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\bar p < \tfrac12\}$.

Part 2.

Agent 2's posterior after $x^n$ is $\mu_n = \mathrm{Beta}(k_n + 1, n - k_n + 1)$.

Agent 1's posterior has density proportional to $p^{k_n}(1-p)^{n-k_n}\mathbf{1}\{p < \tfrac12\}$, so it equals $\mu_n(\,\cdot\,|\,A)$ with $A = (0, \tfrac12)$.

For a probability measure $\mu$, an event $A$ with $\mu(A) > 0$, and any event $B$,

$$
\mu(B \,|\, A) - \mu(B) = \mu(B \cap A)\left(\frac{1}{\mu(A)} - 1\right) - \mu(B \cap A^c),
$$

which lies between $-\mu(A^c)$ and $\mu(A^c)$, with the upper bound attained at $B = A$.

Hence $\|\mu(\,\cdot\,|\,A) - \mu\|_{\mathrm{TV}} = \mu(A^c)$.

The argument in the Beta–Bernoulli section equates $d_n$ with the TV distance between the two agents' posteriors, so

$$
d_n = \mu_n\bigl((\tfrac12, 1)\bigr) = 1 - F_{\mathrm{Beta}(k_n+1,\, n-k_n+1)}(\tfrac12).
$$

Because $\bar p = p$ $P_2$-a.s., $d_n = P_2(\bar p > \tfrac12 \,|\, \mathscr{F}_n)$.

So $\{d_n\}$ is a $P_2$-martingale, and Lévy's upward theorem gives $d_n \to \mathbf{1}\{\bar p > \tfrac12\}$ $P_2$-a.s.

Part 3.

```{code-cell} ipython3
    d = beta_dist.sf(0.5, k + 1, steps_ex - k + 1)   # Pr_2(p > 1/2 | x^n)
    d_final[i] = d[-1]
    ax.semilogx(steps_ex[1:], d[1:], lw=0.8, alpha=0.6,
                color='firebrick' if p > 0.5 else 'steelblue')
ax.set_xlabel('observations $n$')
ax.set_ylabel('$d_n$')
ax.legend(handles=[
    Line2D([0], [0], color='steelblue', lw=2, label=r'$p < 1/2$'),
    Line2D([0], [0], color='firebrick', lw=2, label=r'$p > 1/2$'),
])
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

err = np.abs(d_final - (p_paths > 0.5))
print(f"largest |d_n - 1{{p > 1/2}}| at n = {n_steps_ex}: {err.max():.4f}, "
      f"on the path with p = {p_paths[err.argmax()]:.3f}")
```

Every path ends near $\mathbf{1}\{p > \tfrac12\}$; the paths that converge slowly have $p$ near $\tfrac12$.

Part 4.

With $Q = P_1$ and $P = P_2$, the hypothesis $Q \ll P$ holds, and the theorem gives $d_n \to 0$ $P_1$-a.s.

Because $dP_1/dP_2 = 2\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\bar p < \tfrac12\}$, $P_1$ is $P_2$ conditioned on $\{\bar p < \tfrac12\}$.

So the theorem's conclusion is $d_n \to 0$ $P_2$-a.s. on $\{\bar p < \tfrac12\}$.

The theorem is silent on $\{\bar p > \tfrac12\}$, an event with $P_2$-probability $\tfrac12$ and $P_1$-probability zero.

There $d_n \to 1$: agent 1's prior rules out $p > \tfrac12$, and no data move agent 1's posterior mass above $\tfrac12$.

```{exercise}
:label: mo_ex_gauss

Let $P = \mathcal{N}(0,1)^{\otimes \mathbb{N}}$ and $Q = \bigotimes_m \mathcal{N}(\mu_m, 1)$, as in the Gaussian product-measure example, and let $\sigma_n^2 = \sum_{m > n} \mu_m^2$.

1. Show that $d_n = 2\Phi(\sigma_n/2) - 1$, with $d_n = 1$ when $\sigma_n = \infty$.
2. For $\mu_m = c/m$, show that $d_n \approx c/\sqrt{2\pi n}$ for large $n$.
   Compare the exact and approximate values at $n = 100$ with $c = 2$.
3. For $\mu_m = c/\sqrt{m}$, show that $d_n = 1$ for every $n$, while the distance between the two agents' one-step-ahead forecasts, $\|\mathcal{N}(0,1) - \mathcal{N}(\mu_{n+1}, 1)\|_{\mathrm{TV}}$, converges to zero.

{cite:t}`KalaiLehrer1994Merging` call convergence of forecasts over every fixed finite horizon **weak merging** and convergence of $d_n$ **strong merging**.

Part 3 gives weak merging without strong merging.
```

```{solution-start} mo_ex_gauss
:class: dropdown
```

Part 1.

For product measures, the future is independent of the past, so $d_n$ is the TV distance between $\bigotimes_{m>n} \mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and $\bigotimes_{m>n} \mathcal{N}(\mu_m,1)$.

Suppose $\sigma_n < \infty$.

Then $\sum_{m>n} (1 - \rho_m) \leq \sum_{m>n} \mu_m^2/8 < \infty$, so the two tail products are equivalent, and their likelihood ratio is

$$
L = \exp\!\left(S - \tfrac12 \sigma_n^2\right),
\qquad S = \sum_{m>n} \mu_m x_m .
$$

Under $P$, $S \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_n^2)$; under $Q$, $S \sim \mathcal{N}(\sigma_n^2, \sigma_n^2)$.

Because $L$ is a function of $S$, the TV distance between the tail products equals the TV distance between the two laws of $S$.

Those two normal densities cross at $\sigma_n^2/2$, so

$$
d_n = \Phi\!\left(\frac{\sigma_n}{2}\right) - \Phi\!\left(-\frac{\sigma_n}{2}\right) = 2\Phi\!\left(\frac{\sigma_n}{2}\right) - 1.
$$

Suppose $\sigma_n = \infty$.

If $\mu_m \not\to 0$, the terms $1 - \rho_m = 1 - e^{-\mu_m^2/8}$ do not vanish; if $\mu_m \to 0$, then $1 - \rho_m \geq \mu_m^2/16$ for large $m$.

Either way $\sum_{m>n}(1-\rho_m) = \infty$, Kakutani's theorem makes the tail products singular, and $d_n = 1$.

Part 2.

For $\mu_m = c/m$, $\sigma_n^2 = c^2 \sum_{m>n} m^{-2}$, and $1/(n+1) \leq \sum_{m>n} m^{-2} \leq 1/n$.

So $\sigma_n \approx c/\sqrt{n}$, and $2\Phi(x) - 1 \approx x\sqrt{2/\pi}$ for small $x$ gives $d_n \approx c/\sqrt{2\pi n}$.

The tail sum $\sum_{m>n} m^{-2}$ is the trigamma function $\psi'(n+1)$, which `polygamma(1, n + 1)` computes.

Part 3.

For $\mu_m = c/\sqrt{m}$, $\sum_{m>n} c^2/m = \infty$ for every $n$, so $d_n = 1$.

The one-step forecast distance is $2\Phi(c/(2\sqrt{n+1})) - 1 \to 0$.

For any fixed $k$, the distance between the two agents' forecasts of $(x_{n+1}, \ldots, x_{n+k})$ is $2\Phi\bigl(\tfrac12 (\sum_{m=n+1}^{n+k} c^2/m)^{1/2}\bigr) - 1 \leq 2\Phi\bigl(c\sqrt{k}/(2\sqrt{n+1})\bigr) - 1 \to 0$.

The agents' forecasts of any fixed number of future observations merge, but their forecasts of the entire future never do.

```{code-cell} ipython3
c = 2.0

ax.loglog(n_vals, np.ones_like(n_vals, dtype=float), color='purple',
          lw=2, label=r'$d_n$, $\mu_m = c/\sqrt{m}$')
ax.loglog(n_vals, one_step_sqrt, color='purple', lw=1.5, ls=':',
          label=r'one-step distance, $\mu_m = c/\sqrt{m}$')

ax.set_ylabel('total-variation distance')
ax.legend(fontsize=8)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

      f"approximation = {d_inv_m_approx[n - 1]:.4f}")
```

The one-step distance for $\mu_m = c/\sqrt{m}$ nearly coincides with $d_n$ for $\mu_m = c/m$, because $\mu_{n+1}^2 = c^2/(n+1)$ and $c^2 \sum_{m>n} m^{-2}$ are both close to $c^2/n$.

```{exercise}
:label: mo_ex_zinf

Let $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$ be prior densities on $(0,1)$ with $\pi_2 > 0$ wherever $\pi_1 > 0$, let $P_i = \int_0^1 P_p\,\pi_i(p)\,dp$, and let $\bar p = \limsup_n k_n/n$.

1. Show that $dP_1/dP_2 = \pi_1(\bar p)/\pi_2(\bar p)$ on $\mathscr{F}$, so that $Z_\infty = \pi_1(\bar p)/\pi_2(\bar p)$.
2. For the priors $\mathrm{Beta}(1,8)$ and $\mathrm{Beta}(8,1)$, simulate several paths from $P_2$.
   Compare $\log Z_n$ at large $n$ with $7\log((1-\hat p_n)/\hat p_n)$, where $\hat p_n = k_n/n$, and with $7\log((1-p)/p)$, where $p$ is the coin drawn on that path.
```

```{solution-start} mo_ex_zinf
:class: dropdown
```

Part 1.

Under $P_p$, the strong law of large numbers gives $\bar p = p$ almost surely.

Set $\pi_1/\pi_2 = 0$ where $\pi_2 = 0$.

For every $A \in \mathscr{F}$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_1(A)
&= \int_0^1 P_p(A)\, \pi_1(p)\, dp
= \int_0^1 \mathbb{E}_{P_p}[\mathbf{1}_A]\, \frac{\pi_1(p)}{\pi_2(p)}\, \pi_2(p)\, dp \\
&= \int_0^1 \mathbb{E}_{P_p}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_A\, \frac{\pi_1(\bar p)}{\pi_2(\bar p)}\right] \pi_2(p)\, dp
= \mathbb{E}_{P_2}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_A\, \frac{\pi_1(\bar p)}{\pi_2(\bar p)}\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

The second equality uses $\pi_2 > 0$ wherever $\pi_1 > 0$.

The third replaces $p$ with $\bar p$, which equals $p$ $P_p$-a.s.

So $\pi_1(\bar p)/\pi_2(\bar p)$ is a density of $P_1$ with respect to $P_2$ on $\mathscr{F}$, and {prf:ref}`martingale_convergence` gives $Z_\infty = \pi_1(\bar p)/\pi_2(\bar p)$.

A Laplace approximation explains the convergence of $Z_n$.

The ratio $Z_n = \int_0^1 p^{k_n}(1-p)^{n-k_n}\pi_1(p)\,dp \,/\, \int_0^1 p^{k_n}(1-p)^{n-k_n}\pi_2(p)\,dp$ weights both priors by a likelihood that concentrates near $\hat p_n$, so $Z_n \approx \pi_1(\hat p_n)/\pi_2(\hat p_n)$.

Part 2.

```{code-cell} ipython3
n_ex = 20_000

      f"{'gap to p_hat formula':>21} {'gap to p formula':>17}")
for j in range(6):
    p = rng_z.beta(a2, b2)                     # p drawn from π_2
    data = rng_z.binomial(1, p, n_ex)
    log_Z_n = log_likelihood_ratio(data, a1, b1, a2, b2)[-1]
    p_hat = data.mean()
    gap_hat = log_Z_n - 7 * np.log((1 - p_hat) / p_hat)
    gap_true = log_Z_n - 7 * np.log((1 - p) / p)
    print(f"{p:6.3f} {p_hat:7.4f} {log_Z_n:9.3f} "
          f"{gap_hat:21.3f} {gap_true:17.3f}")
```

At $n = 20{,}000$, $\log Z_n$ differs from $7\log((1-\hat p_n)/\hat p_n)$ by at most $0.10$ across these paths.

That gap is the $O(1/n)$ error of the Laplace approximation, and it is largest on the path with $p$ closest to one, where the derivatives of $\log(\pi_1/\pi_2)$ are large.

The gap between $\log Z_n$ and $7\log((1-p)/p)$ reaches $0.25$, because $\hat p_n$ differs from $p$ by $O(n^{-1/2})$; that gap vanishes as $\hat p_n \to p$.

On the path in the first figure of the lecture, $\log Z_{600} = -4.07$ and $7\log((1-\hat p_{600})/\hat p_{600}) = -4.13$.
